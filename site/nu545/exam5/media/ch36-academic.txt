In cystic fibrosis, the airway problem begins with abnormal epithelial ion transport. The supplied chapter describes defective chloride transport and exaggerated sodium absorption, which deplete airway surface liquid.

Watch the mucus layer lose water and become harder to move. Cilia cannot clear the adherent secretions effectively, and bacteria remain in the airway.

Inflammation then amplifies the problem. Degraded neutrophils release DNA and filamentous actin, adding to mucus viscosity.

Thick secretions obstruct small airways, while chronic inflammation can lead to bronchiectasis and fibrosis. The drawing shows a mechanism, not a scaled measurement of mucus thickness or a prediction for an individual child.

Distinguish this process from newborn respiratory distress syndrome. In that disorder, inadequate surfactant in the immature lung promotes alveolar collapse. Both can impair breathing, but the initiating defect and the affected surface are different.
