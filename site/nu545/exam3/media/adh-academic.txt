When plasma osmolality increases, or circulating volume falls, the body signals for antidiuretic hormone, ADH.

Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus sense the concentration of the plasma.

The hypothalamus also synthesizes ADH.

The hormone is stored in the posterior pituitary, then released into the blood.

Keep those locations separate: synthesis in the hypothalamus, storage and release from the posterior pituitary.

Now follow the blood to the kidney.

ADH acts on the renal collecting ducts and increases their permeability to water.

More water is reabsorbed into the blood.

That is the link between the hormone signal and the fluid response.

Compare that with diabetes insipidus.

In the neurogenic form, there is insufficient ADH.

In the nephrogenic form, the collecting tubules are insensitive to ADH.

Both can leave the patient unable to concentrate urine.

The same result can come from a supply problem or a response problem.
